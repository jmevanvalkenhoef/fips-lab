Recovery is the dead-link timer

FIPS detects a dead link through missing heartbeats. Peers exchange heartbeats, and a link that has been quiet for link_dead_timeout_secs is declared dead. Only then does the node pick a new tree parent and send traffic the other way. Until that moment the node keeps forwarding into the broken link.

The dots sit on a line parallel to the diagonal: rerouting takes the timeout plus a median of – s. That extra is the time between the last heartbeat that got through and the cut, plus the time the reroute itself takes (choosing a new parent and announcing it). So detection accounts for almost all of the delay. With the default timeout of 30 s, a link failure means that for half a minute a sends traffic into a dead link.

A shorter timeout makes rerouting faster, at a cost. It needs more frequent heartbeats on every link, and a congested or lossy link is then declared dead sooner. This experiment only measures the benefit side; the cost side (heartbeat overhead and false "dead" verdicts on lossy links) is the next experiment.

One dot per setting doesn't fit the pattern: in four of the five runs, the very first cut took 8 to 12 s longer than the rest. The rest of this write-up is about why.

Cuts that did not recover

Early in this project, with fast timeouts (heartbeat 2 s, dead after 6 s), two cuts in a row left a unable to reach b for over 90 s, although FIPS had declared the link dead after 7 s and a had already moved to its new parent. Then it stopped happening: – of – cuts later in the same runs got stuck. What the slow and stuck cuts had in common was timing. Each was the first cut after the mesh had started. So I restarted the whole mesh before every cut, and varied how long it ran before the cut.

In a warm mesh the reroute follows the dead-link timer and nothing else. After a fresh start that no longer holds. 15 s after start-up, four cuts in ten take 17 to 41 s and one doesn't recover; 90 s after start-up, – of – don't recover at all. Waiting longer made it worse, so the mesh is not simply still settling. Some state created at start-up is still present and interferes with the reroute.

Where the reply goes

FIPS's logs at debug level show what a is doing. Sessions with no application data are dropped after 90 s, so by the time of a late cut, a has to set up a new end-to-end session to b. Its first handshake message makes it round the ring to b, and b answers, but the answer never reaches a. On its way back, it bounces between d and e until its hop limit runs out, every resend, for as long as the scenario waited.

e is the only node with a wrong idea of where a is: a › b › c, a's position before the cut. Every packet for a that passes e carries a's new coordinates, and e refuses every one of them (its coord_hint_rejected counter stood at 589).

e refuses them on purpose. Coordinates in a passing packet are a claim anyone can make, and a node that believed them blindly could be tricked into routing a destination's traffic anywhere. So FIPS ranks sources: when a node looks a destination up itself and verifies the answer's proof, that entry is verified, and for VERIFIED_TTL_MS, five minutes, it outranks anything passing traffic says. e had looked a up during the lab's convergence check, when a still hung off b. For five minutes after that, e would hold on to a › b › c whatever a did, and nothing in the forwarding path makes e look again.

This explains the earlier results. A warm mesh is past every start-up lookup's five minutes, so hints flow and caches follow the tree. After a fresh start, every node has just verified every other, so the first cut that moves a node in the tree leaves verified, stale entries along the new path. Whether that strands the reply depends on which node holds one. And the 90 s case is worse than the 15 s case because by then the sessions from the check have been dropped, and a new handshake has to cross the loop.

Testing the five minutes

If this explanation is right, it makes two predictions. A cut more than five minutes after start-up should behave like one in a warm mesh. And a stuck cut, left alone, should recover by itself once e's entry expires, about 300 s after the check, whenever the cut was.

Both predictions held. Cut 310 s after a fresh start, – of – got stuck, and they rerouted in a median of – s, as in a warm mesh (the bottom row of the chart above). Then I cut 90 s after start-up again and waited up to 330 s instead of 45. – of – cuts got stuck, and every one came back on its own between – and – s after start-up, whenever the cut was made. That is just after the five-minute mark.

The same thing can happen without a cut. Twice, right after the last heal of a run moved the tree back, a few pairs involving f stayed unreachable for the full 90 s of the next convergence check, on a mesh a few minutes old. Healing a link moves coordinates just as cutting one does.

A five-minute window in which one link change can strand traffic is a long time for a mesh meant to heal itself. The anti-poisoning rule is sound, so a fix should probably keep it and allow a verified entry to be questioned: look the destination up again when a node keeps rejecting hints for it, or when a packet for it runs out of hops. That is the first thing I'll take upstream, with the logs and captures from a stuck cut.

Reconnecting after a heal

Healing the link usually brings it back within a few seconds (median – s), although FIPS does not watch for the link to return. Once a link is declared dead, the node schedules reconnect attempts: the first retry.base_interval_secs (5 s) after the verdict, each next one twice as far out, at 5, 15, 35, 75 and 155 s. A link that heals between attempts stays unused until the next attempt, even though it works.

Every one of the – heals lands on a step. – came before the first attempt and waited for it, a handful just missed it and waited for the second, and the two long cuts from the first runs of this project, healed 83 s after the verdict, missed the fourth attempt by 8 s and waited – s for the fifth, 155 s after it.

Because the interval doubles, after an outage of a few minutes a repaired link can sit idle for about as long again. In these runs the schedule shows no ceiling up to 80 s between attempts. A link that announces itself when it comes back, or a cap on the backoff, would turn that wait into seconds; that is worth taking upstream.

Setup

FIPS is a self-organizing mesh network whose nodes are identified by Nostr keypairs. Nodes build a spanning tree, find each other through bloom-filter announcements and discovery requests, and route along tree coordinates, with no central authority.

FIPS Lab runs FIPS nodes in Docker containers, one container per node and one Docker network per link, so a single link can be impaired or cut without touching the others. Here the mesh is a ring of six nodes, every link 5 ms ± 1 ms. Every pair of nodes has exactly two paths, so when link a–b is cut, traffic from a to b has to go the long way: a → f → e → d → c → b.

  • Fault: both ends of a–b are taken down (ip link set down), like a pulled cable.
  • Reroute time: from the cut until ping b.fips on a gets a reply, probing every ~0.25 s.
  • Declared dead: from the cut until a and b stop listing each other as peers, read from the control sockets once a second.
  • Settings: link_dead_timeout_secs with the heartbeat at a third of it, the ratio of the defaults (10 s / 30 s). Every setting runs on the same node identities, so the spanning tree is the same in every run.
  • Between repetitions: everything is reset and the mesh gets 15 s to settle.
